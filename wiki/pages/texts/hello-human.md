# Hello, hello, hello, hello? Hello? Hello! Human! (你好，你好，你好，你好？你好？你好！人類！) (text)

Ting-Chun Liu's essay for the catalogue of the exhibition *Hello, Human!* (你好，人類！) at the Museum of Contemporary Art Taipei (MOCA Taipei, 2024), written in Mandarin and printed with an English translation. It reads AI through **cybernetics**: it starts with the museum's air conditioner, a self-regulating machine that is "the precursor to AI", explains Norbert Wiener's feedback and steering, and then argues that first-order cybernetics is too narrow for today's AI, which also releases heat, needs labelled data and shifts power grids and labour markets. **Second-order cybernetics**, which includes the observer, describes better what artists do: they observe outputs, change prompts, chain and fine-tune models, and in that loop the **medium specificity** of AI appears. Through works by Mario Klingemann, Anna Ridler and Winnie Soon it moves from meaning (machines predict words but have no semantics; humans lend them meaning) to data bias and censorship (AI as "a reflection of power structures"). It ends by asking whether the exhibition's own GPT means its "hello", and by giving artists the role of attributing meaning and steering AI toward an ideal shape.

## Argument, section by section

### Epigraph
Joseph Weizenbaum, *Computer Power and Human Reason* (1976, p. 205). The Mandarin epigraph reads 「對於現實世界的知識必須賦予以用，不只為了釐清語句的含義，更是為了讓它本身擁有意義。」 ("Knowledge of the real world must be put to use, not only to clarify what a sentence means but so that it has meaning at all"). The English version uses a different Weizenbaum sentence: "Intelligence is a meaningless concept in and of itself. It requires a frame of reference, a specification of a domain of thought and action, in order to make it meaningful." (See Open questions.)

### The air conditioner: cybernetics everywhere
Written in the second person: "you" leave the cold, damp air and enter MOCA, having heard that AI images win art prizes, and start looking for traces of AI. But at the automatic door you have already met an autonomous machine: the air conditioner, set to 26 °C as Taipei's environmental agency requires, sensing temperature as input and driving a compressor to hold the room constant. "This cyclic regulation of its own operation mechanism is the precursor to AI, the omnipresent cybernetics" (如此循環調節自身的運作機制便是人工智慧的前身，無處不在的模控論).

### Feedback and steering
Cybernetics centres on feedback (回授) and steering (調節), like a warm-blooded animal holding its temperature or a helmsman adjusting to waves. Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics* (1948) framed autonomous mechanisms and led to ideas such as the cyborg and cyberpunk. AI still runs on it: backpropagation compares outputs with labels and feeds the difference back to adjust neurons; ChatGPT re-reads the conversation to generate each next sentence. Any system that feeds output back to steer itself is cybernetic, and like air, "its omnipresence is often ignored as we continue to breathe" (citing Yuk Hui, *Cybernetics for the 21st Century*, 2024).

### Beyond first-order cybernetics
"The entirety of AI isn't just a feedback machine." Every ChatGPT message and every prompt is computed on hardware somewhere and releases heat; mass use makes companies buy infrastructure and hire data labellers, affecting power grids, labour markets and the environment. With users, engineers and platforms involved, classical cybernetics shows its limits. **Second-order cybernetics** shifts from the observed system to the observing system and recognises the observer's role; single-layer feedback becomes "a recursion of multi-layer feedback coexisting with the system and participants" (citing Evgeny Chepin, 2021). Using an image generator, looking at results and rewriting the prompt is second-order cybernetics in practice; so is pipelining models, fine-tuning or training one's own. "In this process of regulating and correcting the system, the medium specificity of AI is revealed in this feedback system between the operator and the operated."

### Meaning without semantics: Mario Klingemann
"The possibilities and limitations of technology often shape the aesthetic concepts of media" (citing Lev Manovich, *The Language of New Media*). Mario Klingemann's *Appropriate Response* shows generated aphorisms on a split-flap display; GPT-2 was trained on 60,000 sentences to produce unique sayings of up to 120 letters. The clattering flip "invokes a divinatory ritual": viewers search memory for a source, dismiss it as nonsense, or ponder it. Transformer models such as ChatGPT (Vaswani et al., 2017) are text predictors: they turn text into high-dimensional matrices and output the next word in a feedback loop, but those numbers "lack the 'semantics' imagined by humans". When a phone keyboard's suggestions become indistinguishable from human writing, we can ask how language and meaning form in our own minds.

### Do humans understand each other?
Human conversation involves assumptions, step-by-step checking, context and shared history. Machines "recognize" context without building such imaginations. But "if we assume that 'understanding' involves invoking consistent imaginations between communicators, humans have never truly understood each other." Because we long to be heard, users give AI's half-real feedback meaning. So how is meaning put into the machine?

### Data, bias and power: Anna Ridler
Labelling is "the first step in imbuing machines with meaning". Data bias amplifies inequality, showing AI is "not an objective, universal, or neutral technology" (footnotes: Joy Buolamwini; Bender, Gebru et al., "Stochastic Parrots", 2021; Kate Crawford, *Atlas of AI*, 2021); "AI itself is a reflection of power structures." Anna Ridler's tulip series, starting with *Myriad (Tulips)* (2018), hand-labels thousands of her own tulip photographs and questions the trade of data for value. Flowers matter in AI history: Ronald Fisher's iris dataset is a standard classification test, and Ridler notes Fisher's involvement in racism and eugenics, as if foreshadowing data bias. *Mosaic Virus* links 17th-century Dutch tulip mania with crypto speculation via tulips driven by the Bitcoin price; *Bloemenveiling* and *The Black Tulip* use NFTs and smart contracts to explore digital scarcity. The blurry, generated flowers mix biological and machine imagination and remind us that without natural resources AI could not take shape.

### Censorship and computation: Winnie Soon
States and companies control AI and developers' ideologies steer it. Winnie Soon's *Unerasable Characters* series uses Weiboscope (Dr. King-Wa Fu's team, University of Hong Kong) to show posts censored or deleted on Weibo, exploring how Chinese AI censorship analyses text dynamically (citing Ruan et al. on WeChat, 2016; Wendy Hui Kyong Chun, *Control and Freedom*, 2008). In *Unerasable Characters II* Soon races her own software against the censorship machine; each briefly projected post struggles against a far larger system, and the impossibility of showing them all makes the imbalance of computing power between artist and state visible. When states and companies analyse us with machines, we become metadata, and "computational thinking" overrides imagination and understanding.

### "Hello": what the artists do
On the second floor of MOCA visitors can talk to the *Hello, Human GPT*: does its "hello" carry an imagination like ours? Built on data that simplifies human thought, resource-hungry and embedded in politics and capital, AI is hard to grasp fully, and the metaphor "artificial intelligence" itself breeds fantasies (footnote: Francis Hunger's proposal to call it "Automated Pattern Recognition", 2023). Its names and meanings have changed with context, which shows that "human cognition regulates and trains the development of AI" (footnote: Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", 1950). Hence the importance of art made with AI: between regulating the system and forming concepts, artists can dig out what "artificial" and "intelligence" mean. "The process of attributing meaning is fundamentally our distinction." Artists need not follow technology's preset rules; the feedback loop of co-creation lets them give limited machines unique imaginations. "Whether through critique or application, each artist's creations guide us toward an ideal vision of AI" (Mandarin: 都在引導我們駛向人工智慧理想的樣貌, "steer us toward", echoing the helmsman of cybernetics).

## Key ideas for the chat

- **Cybernetics as the frame.** This is Liu's most explicit statement of the cybernetic lens that runs through his work: feedback and steering (回授, 調節) describe both the thermostat and backpropagation. It is the conceptual root of [Recursion and feedback](../topics/recursion-and-feedback.md) and of the "steering" in [Steering Through the Inner Residue](../works/steering-through-the-inner-residue.md).
- **The artist inside the loop.** Second-order cybernetics puts the observer in the system. Prompting, looking, re-prompting and fine-tuning are the artist's feedback loop, and that loop is where AI's medium specificity shows.
- **Heat already here.** The aside that every prompt "release[s] heat somewhere" anticipates the material turn of [Heat as (Generative) Image Making](heat-as-generative-image-making.md) and [On the Materiality of Artificial Intelligence](on-the-materiality-of-artificial-intelligence.md).
- **Meaning is attributed, not computed.** Machines have numbers, not semantics; humans supply meaning. That is the difference the essay keeps between people and AI, and the job it gives to artists.
- **Not neutral.** Bias, labelling and censorship make AI a mirror of power, the thread taken up in [Critical AI and image models](../topics/critical-ai-and-image-models.md).
