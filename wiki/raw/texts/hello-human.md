為參觀「你好,人類!Hello,Human!」展覽,你逃離寒冷潮濕的空氣進入台北當代藝術館。你聽說
人工智慧生成圖像在藝術比賽得獎,你好奇藝術家如何與機器合作創造新的可能性。於是你開始找
尋人工智慧的蛛絲馬跡。然而一踏入自動門,你已體驗一種自主運作的機器:冷氣機被設定在台北
市環保局規定的26度,重複感測外界溫度為輸入參數,以預設好的程式驅動壓縮機——將更高或更
低溫的空氣輸出到展場中——以維持恆定舒適的溫度;如此循環調節自身的運作機制便是人工智慧
的前身,無處不在的模控論(Cybernetic)。

模控論以回授(Feedback)和調節(Steer)為核心,描述機器或生物系統在預設目的下,回授調節自身
以達成目標的過程。如恆溫動物恆定自身溫度,或船夫依據風浪使舵。諾伯特・維納(Nobert 
Wiener)於1948年出版的模控論成為後世理解自主運作機制的思考框架,也延伸出賽伯格(Cyborg),
賽博龐克(Cyberpunk)等概念。現代人工智慧仍受模控論影響:神經網絡模型訓練的反向傳播
(Backpropagation),通過不斷計算輸出結果與正確標籤資料差異,以回授調節各神經元參數來接近
目標。或ChatGPT重複回溯對話紀錄從而連續生成字句。凡是回授輸入輸出,調節自身以駛向某個
方向的系統,都可用模控論解釋;如同空氣遍布四周,當我們持續呼吸時,我們往往忽略了它的存
在 。

## English version (from the .docx)

Hello, Hello, Hello, Hello? Hello? Hello, Human!
By Ting-Chun Liu / New media artist and researcher

“Intelligence is a meaningless concept in and of itself. It requires a frame of reference, a specification of a domain of thought and action, in order to make it meaningful.”— Joseph Weizenbaum

To visit the Hello, Human! exhibition, you leave behind the cold, damp air and enter the Museum of Contemporary Art, Taipei. You’ve heard that AI-generated images have won art competitions, and you’re curious how artists collaborate with machines to create new possibilities. So, you begin to look for traces of AI. In fact, as soon as you step through the automatic doors, you’ve already encountered a machine operating autonomously: the air conditioner is set to 26 degrees as regulated by the Department of Environmental Protection of Taipei City. It repeatedly senses outdoor temperature as an input parameter, driving the compressor with a pre-set program—either providing warmer or cooler air into the exhibition space—to maintain a constant and comfortable temperature. This cyclic regulation of its own operation mechanism is the precursor to AI, the omnipresent cybernetics.

Cybernetics centers around the concepts of “Feedback” and “Steer,” describing how machines or biological systems self-regulate to achieve goals based on predefined purposes, just as homeothermic animals regulate their body temperature to adapt to environmental changes or a helmsman adjusts the ship’s course in response to shifting waves. Norbert Wiener’s 1948 publication of Cybernetics established a framework for understanding autonomous operating mechanisms in subsequent generations, also giving rise to concepts such as cyborgs and cyberpunk. Modern artificial intelligence continues to be influenced by cybernetics: neural network models employ backpropagation, which involves continuously calculating the difference between the output results and the correct labels, using feedback to adjust the parameters of the neurons to approach the target. Similarly, ChatGPT retraces conversation history to generate sentences continuously. Cybernetics can explain any system that uses feedback to adjust its input and output, directing itself in a certain direction; like air all around us, its omnipresence is often ignored as we continue to breathe.

However, the entirety of AI isn’t just a feedback machine. Every text given to ChatGPT and every prompt used to generate AI art relies on hardware computations that release heat somewhere. When a large volume of generated results enters society, businesses invest in infrastructure and hire data labeling personnel, subsequently impacting the operation and regulation of numerous systems, such as power networks, labor markets, and the natural environment. This illustrates that traditional cybernetics reveals the limitations of its framework in the context of complex systems involving users, engineers, and platform interfaces. In addition, how should we see the role of artists collaborating with AI within this framework? Building on Wiener’s description of the observed system, second-order cybernetics expands the focus on the observing system, acknowledging the role of observers in the functioning of autonomous systems. Cybernetics’s single-layer feedback transforms into a recursion of multi-layer feedback coexisting with the system and participants. The use of an image generator and the iterative process of observing and modifying prompts to achieve an ideal image is an embodiment of second-order cybernetics. Artists use established frameworks to pipeline different models or fine-tune and even train their own models. In this process of regulating and correcting the system, the medium specificity of AI is revealed in this feedback system between the operator and the operated.

The possibilities and limitations of technology often shape the aesthetic concepts of media. Mario Klingemann’s Appropriate Response uses a split-flap display to present a series of seemingly profound yet ambiguous aphorisms. The principle involves training the GPT-2 on sixty thousand sentences to randomly generate unique aphorisms within a limit of 120 letters. The clattering flip of the display panels invokes a divinatory ritual where the pronounced text acts as a catalyst for thought; we seek to find a source within our memory, or we may dismiss it as gibberish or ponder its meaning in depth, creating a stark contrast with the nature of generative AI. AI systems like ChatGPT, based on the transformer model, are a type of text prediction system. They take an input of text, analyze semantics and context, determine and output the next word, and repeat this process in a feedback loop. Such autonomous machines gulp down texts in bulk and analyze their relationships to form high-dimensional matrices that point to each sentence. Thousands of numbers form the basis for computer logic, but they lack the “semantics” imagined by humans. When highly complex text prediction systems like smartphone keyboards produce sentences that are indistinguishable from human-generated text, it prompts us to think about how language and meaning take shape in our minds.

Conversations between people involve multiple assumptions and gradual validations, including contextual judgments and recalling interactions and life experiences with the other party, in order to understand and construct the meaning of the dialogue. We might say that after complex judgment, machines “recognize” the context of the conversation and produce sentences without building many complex imaginations like us. But if we assume that “understanding” involves invoking consistent imaginations between communicators, humans have never truly understood each other. For this reason, we long to be heard and understood, and users endow AI’s seemingly genuine feedback with meaning. Since the feedback of AI is often interpreted by humans, how is meaning then imposed upon the machine?

AI relies on data for training, and data labeling is the first step in imbuing machines with meaning. Data bias amplifies existing inequalities, demonstrating that AI is deeply affected by political, social, cultural, and economic influences and is not an objective, universal, or neutral technology. In other words, AI itself is a reflection of power structures. Anna Ridler’s tulip series, originating from her 2018 work Myriad (Tulips), involves manually tagging a large number of tulip photographs taken by the artist, through which Ridler critiques the modern societal pursuit of efficiency and the exchange of data for value. Flowers have played a significant role in the history of AI development, with the iris dataset created by British statistician Ronald Fisher being a standard test for statistical classification in machine learning. When referencing Fisher, Anna Ridler mentions his significant involvement in racism and eugenics, which may foreshadow the inherent issues of data bias within machine learning. Ridler’s works not only criticize the background of AI development but also respond to different autonomous social systems. Mosaic Virus traces the connection between the 17th-century Dutch tulip mania and contemporary cryptocurrency speculation through tulip variations driven by Bitcoin prices. Bloemenveling and the Black Tulip explore the volatility of the digital art market and the creation of scarcity for digital works through NFTs and smart contracts. The dynamic flowers in her works display the unique blurry aesthetics of generative AI, blending biological and autonomous mechanical imagination. It suggests the convergence of nature and technology as two complex systems while also reminding us that without natural resources, it would be challenging for AI to take shape.

The control of enterprises or governments profoundly influences AI, and developers’ ideologies also sway the direction of technology development. Winnie Soon’s Unerasable Characters Series attempts to unveil the asymmetrical power within digital infrastructures, such as censorship mechanisms. Using the “Weiboscope” from Dr. King-Wa Fu’s team at the University of Hong Kong, she presents posts censored or deleted by China’s Weibo, exploring how AI censorship in China practices dynamic scrutiny through textual analysis. This casts AI from a technological to political power, becoming a central force in maintaining state authority. In Unerasable Characters II, Soon races her self-developed technology against the massive censorship machine, projecting the briefly displayed texts destined for deletion as they struggle against an even larger system, highlighting the meaning that can be assigned within a limited time frame. As the work cannot include all deleted posts, it reflects the imbalance of computational power between the individual artist and the political structure. When states or corporations collaborate with machines to analyze us, we become metadata, an aggregation of whom we’ve spoken with, what products we’ve purchased, and places we’ve visited; our actions and identities are reduced to databases for AI to compute, underscoring computational thinking over imagination or understanding of meaning.

When interacting with the Hello, Human GPT on the second floor of MOCA, does its greeting “hello” imply a similar imagination to our language exchanges? Based on data that simplifies human thought, coupled with massive and resource-intensive computations, AI is deeply embedded in complex political and capital systems, making it inherently difficult to comprehend fully. Not to mention, the metaphorical term “artificial intelligence” leads us to harbor various fantasies and barriers to understanding the system. Looking back in history, we can see that such complex systems have been known by different names, with their meanings evolving and being interpreted differently according to the context. This indicates that human cognition regulates and trains the development of AI. Art that collaborates with AI is particularly significant in this regard. As artists navigate between regulating system use and conceptual creation, they can explore and unearth the significance of both ‘artificial’ and ‘intelligence’ behind the technological system. In the exhibition Hello, Human!, each of our encounters and reflections is a crucial step in understanding AI, and the process of attributing meaning is fundamentally our distinction. Artists’ creations are not confined to the pre-set rules of technological development. The feedback loop in co-creation with artificial intelligence enables them to endow machines of limited capability with unique imaginations, thereby unlocking the door to understanding complex systems for us. Whether through critique or application, each artist’s creations guide us toward an ideal vision of AI amidst the rapid advancement of technology.
